01 / THE BRIEF

Start with the outcome.

“Find a train for Friday after work and get me home by dinner.” Useful delegation begins with a desired result and clear constraints.

The complete task lifecycle

  1. 01

    Start with the outcome.

    “Find a train for Friday after work and get me home by dinner.” Useful delegation begins with a desired result and clear constraints.

  2. 02

    Use chosen personal context.

    Calendar, location, preferences and connected accounts narrow the search. Context stays scoped to the task and the user’s permissions.

  3. 03

    Plan the necessary steps.

    Find options, compare them, check timing and identify missing information. This is a functional explanation; Instinct’s exact planner is undisclosed.

  4. 04

    Check the actual authority.

    A budget is one condition. The passenger, date, merchant, cancellation terms and payment permission matter too. Changed conditions may require another review.

  5. 05

    Execute through services and tools.

    Use connected APIs, computer or phone capabilities and an eligible payment route. Authentication or merchant barriers may still require the user or a service team.

  6. 06

    Follow through to completion.

    Confirm the result, preserve the receipt, update the plan, track changes and resolve exceptions. A successful payment is only part of a completed task.

Still undisclosed

07 / The hard parts

A capable agent still needs clear boundaries.

Once software acts in the world, an error can become a purchase, a commitment, a privacy incident, or more work for the customer.

  1. 01

    Intent is more detailed than a price.

    Correct date, passenger, product variant, delivery address and cancellation terms matter. “Find a table” does not express every possible booking authority.

  2. 02

    A card cannot unlock every merchant.

    Logins, CAPTCHAs, anti-bot controls, unavailable stock and changing websites can stop a task.

  3. 03

    More context means more exposure.

    Connected email, credentials and financial information need clear access, retention, training-use and deletion rules.

  4. 04

    Someone must resolve the problem.

    A failed order, duplicate payment or missing refund needs records, a responsible service team and a practical route to help.

Alfred’s opportunity includes the difficult momentsService quality means doing useful work repeatedly and handling exceptions honestly.
Privacy, incidents and reliability

Instinct’s current policy has a prospective training opt-out, a safety-review exception and specific protection for directly obtained Workspace data. Disconnecting an integration is separate from deleting indexed data. Its terms allocate substantial action responsibility to users.

Reporting includes a disputed premature booking carrying a cancellation fee; an individual incident is not a population failure rate. Prompt injection remains a category risk. Neither a confirmed cross-user breach nor zero human involvement was established here.

Scope, conflicts and the arithmetic

The EU regulation caps covered consumer debit interchange at 0.2% and credit at 0.3%, subject to scope and exceptions. Its debit definition includes prepaid. In this example, gross interchange = spend × 0.002; reward expense = spend × selected reward rate; additional reward funding = the positive difference.

Merchant income can create a recommendation conflict. Make funded offers understandable, verify reward attribution and avoid treating more spending as the default measure of customer success.

Instinct → Alfred

Research and strategic discussion brief · 29 October 2026

Prepared for Max. Alfred is a proposed product. Examples and recommendations below are proposals, not descriptions of a working service, approved partnerships, or licensed financial capabilities.

The conclusion

Instinct is evidence that people want to delegate whole tasks through a familiar conversation. Its apparent strength is the combination of personal context, initiative, execution, and a deliberately simple interface. Strong investor interest and enthusiastic early users support that thesis. Public evidence does not yet establish profitable mass-market adoption.

The proposed Alfred opportunity is credible, but its premise needs updating: Instinct already has substantial payment connectivity. Stripe confirms its Link integration; Instinct announced Link balance on 26 October; Shopify's 28 October release confirms broader Shop Pay checkout through Instinct. A funded agent budget and secure checkout already exist in the category.

The more compelling Alfred ambition is a personal assistant available through Telegram, connected to a bank or neobank relationship, able to act under explicit financial authority, and able to connect purchases, card use, benefits, refunds, and ongoing service. A branded prepaid or debit card can become an important expression of that relationship. Issuing a new card need not be the first dependency of a bank-distributed pilot.

FireFlow is a plausible orchestration foundation. Its public documentation describes several relevant mechanisms. This research did not audit its implementation or test bank-scale deployment. A credible proposal should say what it can supply, what must be integrated, and what still needs proof.

How to read the evidence

  • Primary statement: an official company, investor, developer, regulator, or research source. Useful evidence of what its author says; not automatically independent verification of performance.
  • Reported: credible journalism or analysis, attributed to its source and date.
  • Inference: our interpretation or proposed architecture.
  • Unknown: not established by accessible evidence.

This was public-source research, with three independent investigations covering company/traction, product/privacy, and Telegram/payments. No Instinct account was created, no live transactions were tested, and no new research software was installed. Login-restricted transcripts were not treated as fully read. 3. Why it may be winning

These are strategic inferences from the product and evidence, rather than proven causal explanations.

It removes coordination work. The valuable work is often connecting steps: find the option, compare it, communicate, book, pay, remember, and follow up. A successful assistant compresses that whole burden into one instruction. The benefit becomes emotionally tangible when a task disappears from someone's mind.

It meets users in an existing habit. Messaging is already where people delegate to friends, colleagues, and human assistants. A conversational contact can be easier to remember and use than another dashboard. But joining, connecting accounts, setting authority, and recovering failed tasks still require an interface somewhere.

Context can compound utility. Knowing a preferred airline, delivery address, schedule, and previous choices can reduce repeated explanation. Accurate memory can raise switching costs. Incorrect memory can compound errors. Context therefore creates both advantage and liability.

Trust grows through completed work. Low-risk wins may encourage users to delegate more valuable work. Payment access becomes a behavioural indicator of trust. It is also a selection effect: people who share sensitive information may already be the users who most like the service.

Coordination can generate invitations. If organising a group is easier when other people have an agent, users have a functional reason to invite them. That is a possible network effect; it does not yet establish an enduring, exclusive network or prevent interoperability.

Funding buys capacity and iteration time. The company can invest ahead of demand, improve execution, and develop partnerships. It also faces a very high expectation of growth and competition from firms with existing distribution.

The founder's September interview and its publisher describe rapid invitation growth, trust with cards, transaction volume, and compute pressure. These are useful signals from a selected early-access population. They should not be extrapolated into indefinite daily growth or general-population adoption. Colossus episode and public notes.

What has not been proved publicly

The frequently repeated approximately $1 billion figure refers to annualised transaction activity, not revenue. Sacra reports that Instinct remains free and describes transaction-fee monetisation as an intention. The quality, period, attribution rules, and completed-versus-initiated status of the volume deserve scrutiny. Public founder-reported retention statistics are not a clean substitute for independently defined paid cohorts. Sacra analysis.

Current public materials provide no subscription price table. The terms allow future fees. The founder's later interview is recounted as favouring transaction take rates over advertising; no executed commission schedule or realised take rate was established. Earlier speculation about an advertising model should not be repeated as the settled current business. Episode, terms.

I did not find credible, adequately defined public evidence for current paid retention, net revenue, realised take rate, gross margin, customer-acquisition payback, or cost per successfully completed task. Several automated startup profiles conflict with primary sources; their revenue and founding claims were excluded.

4. The card hypothesis needs a substantial correction

What already exists

  1. Link agent wallet. Stripe's 29 September announcement explicitly names Instinct as a user. It also describes permissioned financial insights and a wider programme of checkout assistance. Features described for the wallet platform should not automatically be assigned to every Instinct account. For example, that announcement initially names Muse for purchase protections. Stripe.
  2. A funded budget. Instinct's official post dated 26 October announces Link balance, added funds, a per-purchase threshold, and a US rollout with New York to follow. Current Link product materials also offer approval of an individual purchase or a budget. This directly weakens “Alfred will be the assistant that can spend within a budget” as a differentiation claim. Instinct announcement, Link product.
  3. Shop Pay. Shopify's 28 October release names Instinct for checkout, including purchases from brands outside Shopify. It describes network tokens that keep underlying card details from the agent. Announced coverage is not proof that every transaction, card, country, or merchant is supported. Shopify release.

Link's balance documentation describes USDC-based funding arrangements, with eligibility, verification, funding-method, and custody terms. A visible dollar budget should not be assumed to be an ordinary bank deposit or equivalent to Alfred issuing a debit card. Custodial and self-custodial product variants exist; their availability should be checked rather than assigned universally. Balance setup, balance FAQ, terms.

What remains a plausible Alfred opportunity

I did not establish a publicly offered Instinct-branded consumer card programme with an issuer relationship, its own cardholder proposition, integrated rewards, and bank-distributed lifecycle service. That is a finding of this search, not proof that no such work is planned.

The distinction worth exploring is between secure access to payment and a coherent customer relationship spanning:

  • agreed spending authority;
  • the customer's card and account;
  • useful benefits and offers;
  • payment and merchant records;
  • delivery, cancellation, return, and refund follow-up;
  • accessible human service when the system cannot resolve a problem.

A card can anchor that relationship. It will not, by itself, solve merchant access, purchase quality, or ambiguous instructions.

5. Core limitations of Instinct and the category

Intent and consent are difficult. “Find a restaurant” and “commit me to a cancellation fee” are different authorities. Budget limits help with amounts, but do not express the correct date, passenger, product variant, refundability, or acceptable contract terms. Earlier reporting includes a disputed booking with a $200 cancellation fee; treat it as a reported incident, not a measured failure rate. Reported example.

Access to a merchant is separate from access to money. Authentication challenges, blocked automation, unavailable stock, unsupported workflows, and changing sites can stop execution. Visa's merchant-facing protocol itself explains the challenge of distinguishing authorised agents from malicious bots. A payment credential does not guarantee agent acceptance or completed fulfilment. Visa Trusted Agent Protocol.

A highly connected assistant concentrates sensitive information. Instinct's current privacy policy provides a prospective model-training opt-out with a safety-review exception, protects directly obtained Workspace data from model training, and distinguishes disconnecting an integration from deleting previously indexed data. Alfred should make retention, deletion, training use, and access scope understandable by default. Current policy.

The legal allocation of responsibility can differ from the emotional promise. Instinct's terms put substantial responsibility for actions on the user, describe the user as the purchaser, and say Instinct is facilitating rather than processing merchant payments. A bank offering a trusted assistant needs a much clearer practical answer to “who will help me when this goes wrong?” Current terms also acknowledge safeguards do not guarantee error prevention. Current terms.

Prompt injection is a structural risk. Webpages, emails, and documents can contain instructions intended to redirect an agent. Model defences help, but independent restrictions on accessible data, destinations, and spending authority remain necessary. Anthropic's research explicitly treats browser-agent prompt injection as unresolved; this is category evidence, not a finding that Instinct suffered a specific breach. Primary research.

Reliability must be repeatable. A striking successful demo can coexist with inconsistent completion. τ-bench's repeated-trial approach is a useful precedent for evaluating stable performance under tool and policy constraints. Shinn co-authored it; that does not establish Instinct's score. Research.

The cost of doing work matters. Background monitoring, long browser sessions, voice, repeated searches, failed attempts, and human rescue all consume resources. Charging on transactions must also fund useful tasks that never generate a purchase. This research does not establish Instinct's cost-to-serve or operating margin.

Proactivity can become work for the user. An assistant that asks about every minor step creates supervision overhead; one that acts too freely can create harm. The product challenge is a usable, revocable delegation policy and selective escalation.

6. What FireFlow could contribute

FireFlow's public materials describe orchestration, persistent memory, and chat functionality. Relevant documented concepts include durable execution, human review pauses, permission boundaries, event history, tool connections, and cost controls. The same homepage marks knowledge graph/RAG and aspects of enterprise identity federation as areas still developing. Treat these as documented product positions, not independently verified bank certifications. FireFlow overview.

More specific documentation supports a narrower technical assessment:

The most important technical boundary

Durable orchestration does not automatically make a third-party purchase happen exactly once. DBOS distinguishes checkpointed workflow recovery, exactly-once database transactions, and steps that can be retried after a crash. If a merchant accepted an order before the assistant recorded the result, retrying may duplicate it. Alfred needs stable operation identifiers, provider-supported idempotency where available, status checks after uncertain responses, and reconciliation. DBOS architecture, TypeScript workflow guarantees.

Likewise, deterministic scheduling does not make an LLM's interpretation correct. Memory is not a payment ledger. Cancelling a workflow does not automatically reverse a card authorisation, merchant order, or contractual obligation.

Proposed division of responsibility

This is an Alfred proposal, not a statement that all these components already exist in FireFlow:

  1. Telegram conversation: understand requests, show progress, deliver receipts and escalation.
  2. Identity and delegation: bind the Telegram user to a verified customer; record who authorised which task, amount, merchant, duration, and conditions.
  3. FireFlow orchestration: plan and route work, preserve progress, wait, recover, and record events.
  4. Execution adapters: merchant APIs, travel/retail integrations, browser or phone operation where justified, and human rescue.
  5. Independent payment rules: evaluate approved authority, available funds, reserved budgets, risk rules, and required authentication.
  6. Issuer/payment partners: provide appropriate card or token credentials, authorisation, settlement, disputes, and regulated responsibilities.
  7. Lifecycle service: track fulfilment, refunds, reward eligibility, reversals, and unresolved cases.

The model proposes actions; enforceable financial rules govern their execution. A slow planning model should not sit in a synchronous card-authorisation decision. As a concrete provider example, Stripe documents a two-second authorisation webhook response deadline. Real-time authorisation.

Controls also have limits. Stripe documents best-effort spend aggregation with possible delay, later tips/fees, and merchant-ID controls in private preview. A contractual promise of an absolute budget ceiling needs more than a generic card-control setting. Spending controls.

My assessment of the FireFlow idea

Architectural fit: promising. Commercial and operational readiness: unproved by this research. It could reduce the amount of infrastructure a team needs to assemble, particularly for long-lived work and reviewable execution. It cannot substitute for distribution, merchant capabilities, issuer acceptance, reliable task behaviour, or service operations.

Before claiming scale for a bank, measure completed tasks per active customer, cost per successful task, latency, intervention rate, unsafe-action rate, recovery correctness, isolation, revocation, and peak-load behaviour. Obtain deployment and commercial licence terms: the docs describe BUSL-1.1 source availability, which should not be translated into an unrestricted free commercial-use promise.

7. Telegram is a strong initial surface with real boundaries

Telegram supports bots, Mini Apps, and provider-based physical-goods/service payments. That makes chat, approvals, budget views, receipts, and service interactions plausible. Its merchant invoice API is a way to accept payments; it is not an issuing platform or a general mechanism for an agent to pay arbitrary external merchants. Physical payments, Mini Apps.

Ordinary Telegram bot conversations do not provide the end-to-end encryption of Secret Chats. The bank and Alfred must be explicit about what passes through chat. Proposed approach: use chat for requests and suitably minimised updates; use bank-controlled or provider-hosted authenticated screens for sensitive identity, credentials, mandate changes, and required financial approvals. A Mini App improves interface flexibility but does not automatically confer bank-grade identity or secrecy. Telegram FAQ, Mini App validation.

Selling Alfred's own digital service inside Telegram raises a separate commercial constraint: Telegram requires Stars for digital goods and services sold there. Physical purchases have different rules. A bank-sponsored entitlement needs product and policy review; do not assume it can use any billing arrangement merely because the bank pays. Digital payments rules.

Telegram should be the chosen first experience. Keeping the backend portable preserves the option to serve a bank's own app later. Some banks will value Telegram's reach; others may decline it as their primary customer channel. That must be tested with the target buyers.

That recommendation is a proposed route to validation. Max has not selected whether Alfred should primarily be an independent consumer brand, a co-branded bank benefit, or infrastructure sold under a bank's identity. This is the most consequential question before writing the short deck.

An initial proposition could centre on busy customers who repeatedly arrange travel, local services and household purchases. 10. What the card and rewards should do

The consumer card and the agent's execution credential need not be identical. A product could offer a normal physical/virtual card while using separate constrained credentials for assistant purchases. Whether those credentials can be linked to the same account and rewards proposition depends on the issuer, network, wallet and contractual arrangements.

There is an especially relevant integration trap: Link's terms warn that some separately created payment methods can lose offers, benefits or rewards attached to the underlying card. Alfred must verify reward attribution, merchant identification and the treatment of wallet-funded purchases before promising that the assistant optimises the customer's existing benefits. Link terms.

The card can give Alfred a consented signal of ordinary spending as well as an execution route. Transaction feeds do not automatically reveal every basket item or fulfilment state; merchant, order, and receipt connections may still be needed.

The proposed loop is:

express an intent → agree authority → find a suitable outcome → execute → reconcile settled spend → apply eligible benefits → follow up.

Rewards should serve the customer: relevant benefits, automatic application of eligible offers, and clear progress toward something they already value. Buying more should not be the default measure of success. Merchant-funded recommendations need understandable disclosure and a ranking policy that protects the assistant's credibility.

Economics that constrain the vision

For in-scope EU consumer card transactions, the regulation caps debit interchange at 0.2% and credit at 0.3%, subject to its scope and exceptions. Prepaid does not create a general escape from debit rules. EU regulation, official summary.

Illustration, not a forecast: €1,000 of monthly spend at 0.2% creates €2 of gross issuer interchange before programme costs and revenue sharing. A 1% reward costs €10. That leaves an €8 gap before assistant execution, support, fraud, issuer/processor costs, and profit. An interchange-only plan does not support that reward promise.

Possible funding sources are a paid bank benefit, consumer membership, merchant-funded offers, affiliate/booking commissions, or a negotiated share of measurable bank value. Each has distribution costs, restrictions, attribution uncertainty and potential conflicts. They should be modelled separately.

A useful operating formula is:

Contribution = collected membership/bank fees + net merchant income + net programme revenue − rewards − task execution − partner fees − support − expected losses.

Price around active use and completed outcomes; do not assume every registered customer can economically receive unlimited background work. A bank may fund a premium benefit before it can support free service across its full customer base.

11. Issuance, geography and responsibility

No launch jurisdiction has been chosen. Spain, the UK, the EEA and the US are not interchangeable product environments. Link's agent features, consumer issuing, commercial issuing and merchant checkout each have their own coverage.

Current Link developer documentation lists agent payments for US and Canadian customers and financial insights for US customers. That general wallet coverage is separate from the October announcement's narrower initial balance rollout. A business based elsewhere does not gain worldwide consumer eligibility from being able to integrate the API. Wallet availability.

For example, Stripe's current issuing overview distinguishes commercial issuing coverage across several markets from US consumer issuing. Availability of a commercial card API in Europe does not establish a European consumer Alfred programme. Issuing overview.

A concrete programme needs an appropriate licensed issuer/bank or EMI relationship; agreed cardholder eligibility and identity processes; funding and safeguarding/deposit treatment; fraud and authentication; authorisation/settlement reconciliation; refunds, disputes and complaints; and explicit responsibility for agent errors. The applicable obligations depend on the chosen product and market. These are design dependencies, not a conclusion that Alfred itself needs a banking licence.

An existing-card route is also conditional: network enablement, tokenisation eligibility, approved agent onboarding, partner agreement and merchant acceptance must line up. A public network announcement is not a promise that Alfred can start transacting immediately. Issuer documentation also allows late, excess and forced captures in specified cases, so an approved spending policy is not a universal guarantee that settlement can never exceed an initial amount. Prefer well-defined online tasks first, with reconciliation and clear recourse. Visa developer guide, issuer transaction lifecycle.

The privacy and outsourcing model also needs to match the partner bank. Permission to see transactions should not silently become permission to ingest every email or train a general model on customer context. Product-specific legal assessment comes after selecting geography, partners and operating roles.

12. A vision direction for the later short deck

This is a proposed narrative for discussion, not an approved deck or product specification.

Alfred: a personal assistant for everyday life, connected to your money.

The emotional promise is time, reduced mental load, and control. Telegram makes it easy to reach. The financial connection makes it useful when a task requires spending. The card makes the relationship tangible. The orchestration makes commitments traceable and recoverable.

Suggested six-slide arc:

  1. Life has become an endless list of things to organise. Make the problem recognisable in a day-in-the-life scene.
  2. Meet Alfred. Show one Telegram conversation resolving an ordinary but multi-step need. Label it an illustrative future experience.
  3. Give Alfred a budget and a clear brief. Connect conversation, spending authority, and a card; make control understandable.
  4. Everyday value that remembers you. Show relevant preferences, benefits and follow-through without promising universal savings or perfect autonomy.
  5. A new relationship between people and their bank. Explain why the bank participates: useful engagement, primary-card relevance, a premium benefit and measurable customer outcomes. These are pilot hypotheses.
  6. The opportunity to build it together. Explain FireFlow's proposed role and the initial partner/pilot ask once audience and business route are selected.

Keep detailed orchestration and operating assumptions in backup material. The main story should show the user experience and the bank opportunity. Avoid a guaranteed rewards percentage, invented traction, claims of universal acceptance, or claims that the current platform already meets every bank requirement.

15. What would change the recommendation

  • A target bank will not permit Telegram, or the desired customers rarely use it.
  • The essential task success rate remains low after connecting appropriate providers.
  • Customers will not grant the permissions required for a useful first experience.
  • The partner cannot provide suitably scoped payment authority or clear recourse.
  • Task/support costs exceed the benefit a bank or customer will pay for.
  • A new card adds activation friction while an existing-card route satisfies the same needs.
  • A competing platform delivers comparable completion quality and benefits through channels customers already trust.

Conversely, evidence of repeated successful tasks, controlled spending, low supervision, attractive service costs, and bank demand would strengthen the case far more than a large general agent market estimate.

The next discussion should establish who owns Alfred's customer relationship: Alfred as an independent consumer brand with banks behind it, or Alfred as the assistant a bank gives to its customers. The eventual pitch can be ambitious in either case, but the buyer, economics, distribution and final ask change.
